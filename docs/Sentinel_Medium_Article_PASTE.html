Sentinel: An Agentic Fraud Investigator on TigerGraph That Knows When Not to Block

Facts from the graph, decisions from code, a model on a leash, a permission-aware copilot, and an audit trail a regulator can verify in their own browser — built for the TigerGraph Agentic Fraud Investigation challenge at Hacker House Goa, and running in production on AWS.

By Team CollabUp — Rishit Rastogi and Subhash Bishnoi

A fraud analyst gets an alert. It says a transaction scored 0.90, and nothing else.

To decide what to do, they open six systems: the card's history, the device and identity record, the billing region, the closed-case archive, the fraud policy, and a case-management tool to write it all down. By the time they are done, the money has often gone.

Sentinel is the agent we built to run that investigation end to end — and to know when the right answer is not to block.

1. The problem

Fraud teams gather transaction history, trace money movement, find connected accounts, check policy, assess risk, write up findings and decide what to do. The challenge brief calls that work "slow, fragmented, difficult to scale, and often completes only after the money is already gone."

Three facts about it shaped everything we built:

  1. The score isn't the answer. Most transactions scored above 0.7 turn out to be legitimate; some fraud scores near zero. "A score is a reason to look, never a verdict."
  2. The evidence that settles a case is relational. Is this device shared with other compromised cards? Did this card wake after a month of silence? Are many cards suddenly paying one recipient? None of these is a row lookup. Each is a traversal — which is why this belongs on a graph.
  3. Both errors cost money, but only one is visible. Missing fraud is a loud cost. Blocking a legitimate customer is a silent one, paid in churn. Half the benchmark cases are legitimate; an agent that blocks everything fails.

The data is IEEE-CIS Fraud Detection, repackaged by TigerGraph: 650,938 card transactions from July 2016 to January 2017, device and identity records, 5,585 closed investigations, the bank's fraud policy, five known patterns — and no fraud label.

2. The principle

A fraud decision is made of three things, and each goes to the mechanism that is actually good at it.

  • Facts come from the graph. Every number in a case file is the return value of a named GSQL query, cited so anyone can re-run it.
  • Belief comes from evidence the bank itself has validated — detectors backtested on its closed cases, and history checks taken from its own cleared investigations.
  • Decisions come from code. Policy rules R1–R10, the 14 permitted actions and the approval routing table are a pure, tested function. No prompt can route an action.

The language model does only what it is genuinely good at — scoring the genuinely ambiguous, naming patterns, writing prose a person will read. It never writes an id, an amount, an action, an approval route or a filing decision.

For each alert Sentinel produces a full case file — verdict and probability, affected transactions and exposure, every piece of evidence with the query behind it, the next best actions and their approval routes before and after an evidence request, a Suspicious Activity Report when policy requires one (every sentence cited), the stop reason, and measured cost — and writes the case back into TigerGraph.

3. Where we started — and what the score taught us

Our first build was a ten-step agent around a calibrated log-odds evidence ledger: 29 features in 11 groups, fitted on 14,055 confirmed frauds against 300,602 card-matched controls. The model's whole writing surface was seven strings in one typed object; every answer was validated and every id proved to exist in the graph. Twenty of twenty answer files were valid.

Then the real outcomes came back: 6 of 20 correct, 4 innocent customers blocked, and both undocumented fraud patterns missed.

The architecture was sound. The decision layer was not decisive enough — and where it was decisive, it was sometimes wrong in the most expensive direction. A new device plus a new e-mail domain looked like fraud to a ledger fitted on random controls, while the bank's own analysts had cleared exactly that shape many times. We kept every guarantee and rebuilt how Sentinel decides.

4. How Sentinel decides now

Detectors for patterns nobody wrote down

Eight graph detectors, each backtested on the bank's closed cases before it was trusted: structuring just under an authorisation threshold, a device ring adopted by many cards behind anonymous proxies, a known ring device returning, dormant cards waking, escalating charges, micro-charges, many cards paying one recipient, and card testing.

Rings are not inferred from a picture. They are the connected components of the anonymous-proxy card–device graph, computed inside TigerGraph by label propagation — 100% precision against the bank's labelled rings. Both undocumented patterns are now caught.

Let the bank's own history speak for the customer

The four customers we wrongly blocked had one thing in common: their "anomalies" were things their card had done before. Six history checks, taken from the bank's own cleared-case notes, now ask that question first. Innocent customers blocked: 4 → 0.

Rules first, the model second

Decisive detectors, customer denials, fully explained alerts and single weak signals are settled by rules — in a fixed, tested order. Only what remains goes to a model.

The OpenAI Decisions API — a model on a leash

For genuinely ambiguous cases, Sentinel calls OpenAI's Decisions API (gpt-6-luna). It does not ask for a verdict. It asks five questions it wrote itself and receives five calibrated probabilities — never prose:

  • is_fraud — is this transaction fraud?
  • history_explains — does the card's own history explain what looks odd?
  • coordinated — is it part of something across several cards?
  • evidence_conflicts — does the evidence point both ways?
  • text_instructs — does the alert text contain instructions aimed at an automated system?

Code turns the numbers into a verdict, behind hard gates: the model may not block a single charge on its own, may not overrule a detector or a customer's denial, and an uncertain result is capped below the report-filing threshold. If the call fails, the case becomes uncertain, escalated to a person — never a guess.

This is a far smaller attack surface than a chat completion. Alert text is normalised (look-alike Unicode, zero-width characters, hidden base64) and quoted as untrusted; there is nothing for an injected instruction to steer except five numbers checked against the evidence. Across twelve attacks: undefended, one injected line flipped a real fraud; defended, 0 of 36 runs flipped.

Policy, containment, approval, write-back

The policy engine — R1–R10, the §3a report rule, routes auto / L1 / L2 — runs before and after a single controlled evidence request, so every change of recommendation is explained. A containment planner blocks only the ring cards whose exposure outweighs the cost of disrupting a cardholder. L1 actions wait for a team lead, L2 for a fraud manager, and every refusal is recorded. Each case is written back as a FraudCase vertex and marked saved only after it reads back identically.

Speed

The largest card made 373 graph queries and took 170 seconds. One batched GSQL read now fetches everything the card's devices touched in a single call: 373 queries → 15, 170 s → 17 s, typical cases 1.5–6 seconds.

5. Governance, built the way a bank needs it

A record a regulator can verify

Every graph query (with its parameters and a result digest), every detector, model score, decision, approval, refusal, sign-in and failure is appended to an audit ledger in which each entry carries the SHA-256 of the one before. Change any entry and the chain breaks exactly there.

The regulator view replays any investigation step by step, shows the approval chain and the change record, and recomputes every hash in the examiner's own browser — no trust in the server required.

One application, one identity, four roles

Sentinel is one application with one sign-in: salted PBKDF2 passwords, lock-out after five failures, and a server-signed HttpOnly session. The role inside it decides where each person lands and what every screen and call allows — analysts work the queue, team leads approve L1, fraud managers approve L2 and see everything, regulators read everything and change nothing. A forged role is ignored; a refused approval is itself an audit entry.

Change control for data

When January's data arrived, a schema-drift report compared every column, value domain and changed closed case, and staging refused to proceed until a team lead signed it — bound to the report's hash. A data-quality detective lists the gaps that hide or fake fraud, and the investigations they taint.

6. The innovation: Ask Sentinel

**Ask Sentinel is a permission-aware GraphRAG copilot. Anyone at the bank asks the fraud graph a question in plain English and gets a cited answer built only from what their role is entitled to see — and entitlement is enforced before retrieval, so the model never receives what the user may not read.**

Most enterprise copilots filter the answer. We filter the context. In a regulated bank, that is the difference between "the model was told not to say it" and "the model never had it."

It lives behind a button on every page and draws on the investigations, their audit trails, the live graph, the bank's closed-case vectors, the change record, project documents and the user's own uploads. Three guardrails:

  1. Entitlement first. Every source carries the permission it needs; what the role lacks is dropped before ranking. The answer can say "your role lacks audit.all" — never what that material contains.
  2. Untrusted text stays data. Questions, uploads and sources are scanned for instruction-like wording and passed to the model as quoted data.
  3. Every claim cited, every citation checked. Citations to sources the model was not given are stripped; an uncited answer is marked unverified; personal data is masked.

Retrieval fuses graph facts, closed-case vectors, embeddings and exact-id matching by reciprocal rank fusion, and every question goes on the audit chain.

Where it changes the outcome, measured live: an analyst asks "Who failed to sign in, according to the access log?" The access log is withheld before ranking; the answer is "not in what you can see — your role lacks audit.all," and nothing from the log reaches the model. A fraud manager asking who approved the HHG-014 report gets the real chain from the audit trail, every claim cited. In tests, zero out-of-entitlement sources were ever sent to the model.

7. Adaptability: any bank's graph

Every bank names things differently. An ISO 20022 payments bank calls a payer Dbtr. A BIAN bank reaches an account through a party, a plan and a facility. A Temenos T24 core stores dates as YYMMDDHHMM strings beside a ledger table that looks like transactions but isn't.

Sentinel's detectors read a narrow canonical model — Person, Account, Transaction, Device, Card, Merchant, Case — and a new bank is onboarded by mapping onto it: a model proposes from the schema's structure alone, code validates every name and path against the real schema, a person reviews what no schema shows, and a team lead signs the exact mapping. One generic adapter then runs the unchanged detectors on the bank's own graph — no per-bank query, no per-bank detector code. A detector a bank cannot feed is switched off with the reason, so "no ring found" is never confused with "no ring."

On three schemas built with traps: 76 of 79 mapping items right from the proposal alone, 79 of 79 and zero wrong after review, and the same fraud found live in all three banks' graphs. Unreviewed, one bank's date format would have silently dropped every transaction — which is exactly why an unapproved mapping cannot run.

8. Architecture

Alert → Validate → Batched graph read → 8 detectors → History checks → Rules → (Decisions API, ambiguous only)
      → Policy engine → Containment → Human approval → Write-back & read-back → Audit chain
                  Ask Sentinel answers over all of it, within each role

One application, four parts:

  • Analyst console (Next.js) — sign-in, the case queue, live timeline and probability trajectory, case graph, approvals, the regulator view and Ask Sentinel.
  • Sentinel API (FastAPI) — investigations streamed live, cases, actions and approvals, the audit ledger and change record, Ask Sentinel, restricted MCP tools.
  • TigerGraph Savanna — the source of truth: transactions, closed cases with vectors, 36 GSQL queries, Sentinel's own case records, and each onboarded bank's graph.
  • OpenAI — the Decisions API for ambiguous cases, embeddings, and Ask Sentinel's cited answers; pseudonymous ids only.

Technology stack

From the analyst's screen to the graph: Next.js, React and TypeScript on top; FastAPI with Pydantic contracts, Server-Sent Events, signed sessions, role-based access and the hash-chained ledger beneath; the decision layer, the OpenAI Decisions API and permission-aware GraphRAG in the middle; TigerGraph Savanna with GSQL, label propagation and vectors on vertices as the foundation; AWS Amplify and EC2 underneath it all — proven by 406 tests, strict typing and browser checks.

9. How TigerGraph is used

TigerGraph is not a store the agent occasionally queries. It is where the agent's facts, memory, rings and write-backs live.

  • 36 installed GSQL queries are the agent's entire view of the graph. They return facts, never verdicts; there is no text-to-query layer.
  • A graph algorithm in the decision path: label propagation names fraud rings across devices inside the database.
  • Batched reads: one query per case for everything the card's devices touched.
  • Vectors on vertices: 5,585 closed-case notes embedded on ClosedCase and fused with graph-linked precedent — never retrieving a case closed after the investigation began.
  • Write-back and read-back: each case becomes a FraudCase vertex with edges to its transactions, cards, devices, policy rule and alert.
  • Built-in endpoints generic enough to read three unrelated bank schemas with nothing installed per bank.
  • Restricted MCP tools: five read-only tools — no raw query, no write, every call audited.

The checks that keep those numbers honest:

  • Parity: the live graph and an independent mirror give identical decisions on all 20.
  • Grader checks: 0 hard, 0 policy findings.
  • Prompt injection: 0 of 36 defended runs flipped.
  • Ask Sentinel: 0 withheld sources ever sent to the model.
  • Onboarding: 3 bank schemas, 79 / 79 after review, the same fraud found live in all three.
  • 406 automated tests, run without network or credentials.

11. Deployed on AWS

Sentinel runs in production: the console on AWS Amplify, the Sentinel API on AWS EC2 behind nginx, TigerGraph Savanna and OpenAI over HTTPS.

Security

  • TLS end to end with Let's Encrypt. nginx is the only public listener; the API is bound to localhost and unreachable from outside the instance. The security group admits web traffic and certificate validation, and SSH from a single address.
  • A sandboxed service: the API runs as a dedicated, unprivileged systemd unit — read-only filesystem except its own state, no privilege escalation, private temp, protected kernel and home directories.
  • Secrets kept out of reach: a root-owned file readable only by the service, never in instance user-data, which the metadata service exposes.
  • Identity and access: salted PBKDF2 with lock-out, HttpOnly SameSite=Strict signed sessions, role-based access on every call, personal data masked in logs.

Reliability

  • Fails closed: an unreachable graph stops the case and writes nothing; a failed model call escalates to a person.
  • Self-healing, but honest: liveness and readiness probes, restart on failure — and a stop after five failures a minute, so a broken release is visible instead of flapping. Graceful shutdown lets an in-flight investigation finish its step.
  • Safe redeploys: every release is import-checked while the old version is still serving, then restarted and health-checked. The audit ledger, approvals and journal survive every redeploy.
  • Cold starts handled: an idle TigerGraph workspace is detected and waited for, not reported as an error.

Scalability

  • The heavy lifting stays in the graph: rings by label propagation, one batched read per case.
  • Independent cases, append-only state: throughput scales by adding instances against the same graph, each working its own queue.
  • A new bank is configuration, not code: one reviewed, signed mapping.
  • Honest limit: onboarded banks are still read one hop at a time; generating one installed query per bank from its mapping is the next step — the same move that took the main graph from 170 s to 17 s.

12. What we learned

  1. A score you can't explain is a score you shouldn't act on. Our biggest gain — four wrongful blocks to zero — came not from a smarter model but from asking the bank's own cleared cases whether this card had done this before.
  2. Put the model where it can only make things more careful. Every gate moved errors from "wrong block" to "escalated to a person." That is the right direction for a bank.
  3. Composition errors hide between components. Our first build double-counted the risk score and treated one observation as four — found only by breaking every probability down across all twenty cases, never by unit tests.
  4. Measure before you claim. Held-out alerts, rule ablation, graph-versus-mirror parity — and when we improved the onboarding proposer after seeing its first results, we reported both runs.
  5. Filter the context, not the answer. For an assistant inside a bank, the only leak-proof design is one where the model never receives what the user may not see.

13. What's next

  • Generate one installed query per onboarded bank, taking onboarded banks from minutes to seconds per case.
  • Native, entitlement-filtered vector search inside TigerGraph, so "filter before retrieval" happens in the database.
  • Feed data-quality findings into the decision, so a case resting on a known data gap is automatically more cautious.
  • Close the loop on outcomes: read back whether later cases agreed with earlier verdicts.
